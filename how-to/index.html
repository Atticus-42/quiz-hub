Give a one-minute brief

Put the main point first and check that the listener knows what to do next.

Open the steps ＋

Use this when a classmate or project team needs a quick update with a decision or action.

  1. Decide what the listener needs to do

    Pick one outcome before you speak. For example, ask your group to confirm who will bring the printed handouts.

  2. Open with the request

    Say what you need in the first sentence. Try “Please confirm who will bring the handouts to tomorrow’s practice.”

  3. Give just enough context

    Add the facts the listener needs to act, such as the number of copies and when they’re needed. Use familiar words and explain any acronym that matters.

  4. Choose a channel that fits

    Use a quick message for a simple update. If people need to discuss the decision, speak live and leave time for questions.

  5. Ask for a reply you can check

    Ask who will do it and by when. Listen to the answer, correct any mismatch, and confirm the agreed action.

Done when

The listener can repeat the action, who owns it, and when it’s due.

Watch for: Treating silence as confirmation or burying the request under background.

Coach one behavior at a time

Turn an observation into a small change the other person can practice.

Open the steps ＋

Use this after a classroom brief, rehearsal, or routine team task, while both people can still recall what happened.

  1. Name the moment

    Choose one specific point in the task. Try “When we switched to the last slide in today’s rehearsal…”

  2. Describe what you saw

    Say what happened in terms the other person could verify. For example, “The due date was on the slide but didn’t come up in the explanation.”

  3. Ask how it went for them

    Invite their account first. Listen for context you missed and explain the effect you observed, such as two people leaving with different dates.

  4. Agree on a small adjustment

    Ask what they would do next time. Set a practice goal tied to the issue, such as saying the date aloud before opening questions.

  5. Look for the adjustment next time

    Check that behavior at the next rehearsal. Acknowledge when they make the change and close the loop.

Done when

Both people can name the behavior to practice and how they’ll tell whether it improved.

Watch for: Stacking several criticisms together or guessing why someone acted a certain way.

Plan backward from a deadline

Turn one fixed deadline into a short timeline the group can actually follow.

Open the steps ＋

Use this classroom exercise when your team has a fixed presentation time and several tasks to finish first.

  1. Start with the finish time

    Write the deadline and what counts as ready. For a 3 p.m. presentation, the deck is open, roles are clear, and the group can start on time.

  2. Put the last check just before it

    Decide when the group needs to rehearse or open the deck together. Leave time to make corrections before the presentation.

  3. Work back through dependencies

    Place tasks in reverse order. The rehearsal needs a merged deck, and the merged deck needs everyone’s slides.

  4. Protect the team’s preparation time

    Try the lesson’s one-third/two-thirds split as a classroom rule of thumb. For a 90-minute window, aim to give the group its direction in about 30 minutes so it has about 60 to prepare.

  5. Check and share the timeline

    Give each milestone one owner. Compare the times in the schedule and on the slides before sharing a single version.

Done when

Every milestone has a time and owner, and the group can point to the time reserved for rehearsal or corrections.

Watch for: Using most of the available time to perfect the plan before the group can start.

Reconcile a training inventory

Compare an authorized equipment count with the record and flag any difference for follow-up.

Open the steps ＋

Use this for a classroom kit or demo equipment you’re authorized to count. Follow the custodian’s inventory process.

  1. Agree on what is being counted

    Ask the custodian which items and approved record are in scope. For a practice run, use mock entries for a classroom kit.

  2. Count what you can verify

    Work through the list with the responsible person. For each line, compare the recorded count with what you can physically verify.

  3. Record the difference plainly

    Keep the expected and observed counts separate. For example, record “6 listed, 5 counted, 1 unresolved” so the gap stays visible.

  4. Flag the discrepancy

    Bring the difference and any supporting records to the custodian. Distinguish what you verified from explanations that still need checking.

  5. Follow the authorized update process

    Keep the item open until the custodian resolves it and updates the record through the proper process. Confirm the count and the final disposition agree.

Done when

Someone reviewing the inventory can tell what was counted, what is unresolved, and who is following up.

Watch for: Changing the record to make the count match before the discrepancy is resolved.

Check before sharing a file

Work through a short sharing decision using a fictional classroom document.

Open the steps ＋

Use this classroom exercise to practice the checks that come before sending information. Work with a mock document and fictional recipients.

  1. Read the document’s markings

    Start with the handling information provided in the exercise. If it is unclear, mark the decision as pending and ask the document owner what rules apply.

  2. Check who actually needs it

    List the intended recipients and the work each needs the file for. Check the authorization required by the exercise for each recipient.

  3. Confirm permission and channel

    Identify who can authorize the transfer and which channel the exercise permits for that information. Wait for clarification if either is missing.

  4. Record the decision

    Use the practice log to capture what would be shared, with whom, under whose authorization, and through which channel.

  5. Practice the escalation

    If the scenario says the file went to the wrong recipient, identify the designated reporting contact and flag the suspected disclosure promptly.

Done when

You can point to the recipient’s need, the authorization, the approved channel, and the reporting contact.

Watch for: Assuming that a familiar recipient or a convenient chat channel is enough to authorize a transfer.

Write one cyber risk entry

Connect an asset, a threat, and a weakness to a concrete effect and next action.

Open the steps ＋

Use this classroom exercise for a fictional group workspace, such as a shared copy of the presentation slides.

  1. Choose one asset

    Name the file, service, or device and what the group relies on it for. For example, the group needs its shared slide deck to present on Friday.

  2. Describe what could go wrong

    Write a threat and the weakness that makes it possible. Accidental edits are the threat. Broad edit permissions are the weakness.

  3. Describe the effect

    Say what changes if it happens. Slides may become inaccurate, which affects integrity, or the group may lose access to a usable deck, which affects availability.

  4. Judge likelihood and impact

    Explain how plausible it is given the scenario and how much it would disrupt the group. Mark missing evidence as an assumption.

  5. Pick an action and a check

    Suggest one step that addresses the weakness, such as giving most participants view access, and say how the authorized owner could verify the result.

Done when

Your entry reads as one clear chain from asset and weakness to impact, with an action that reduces the risk.

Watch for: Listing a weakness without explaining its effect or assigning a precise score without supporting evidence.

Read a modeled radio link

Separate terrain clearance, predicted received level, and margin when reading a saved result.

Open the steps ＋

Use this for a classroom interpretation exercise with a saved model or a fictional path between points A and B.

  1. Identify the model inputs

    Write down the band, transmitter power, antenna heights, gains, and losses shown for the run. Keep these alongside the result so it’s clear which settings produced it.

  2. Read the path profile

    Check the legend and distinguish a clear optical path from terrain in the path. Notice whether the model also reports clearance around the direct path, called Fresnel clearance.

  3. Read the level and margin separately

    Received level is shown in dBm. Subtract the model’s receiver threshold from the predicted received level to find the margin in dB. In a fictional run, −90 dBm against −100 dBm gives 10 dB of margin.

  4. Explain the result in a sentence

    Say whether terrain blocks the direct path and whether the model still predicts a usable signal. The worked example shows both outcomes can occur together.

  5. Mark it as a prediction

    Keep the model result and any authorized real-world check in separate fields. For this exercise, leave the field result blank if all you have is a screenshot.

Done when

You can explain the predicted margin and terrain clearance separately and name the inputs used.

Watch for: Reading a modeled pass as measured coverage or comparing results without checking their settings.

Compare current and target safeguards

Use a small NIST profile to turn a gap into an action your group can track.

Open the steps ＋

Use this classroom exercise to compare what a fictional team does today with what it wants to achieve.

  1. Set the boundary

    Pick one workflow, such as how your class group accounts for shared presentation files. Write down what is in scope and any assumptions you made.

  2. Gather the information you have

    List the mock rules, practices, roles, and tools in the scenario. Use those details when describing the current state.

  3. Pair current with target

    Pick one outcome from the framework. For example, for data inventory, write “The group knows the slide deck exists” under current and “The group keeps a list of designated shared files” under target.

  4. Write down the gap

    Explain what has to change and which part matters most. Turn that difference into one action with an owner and a date to review it.

  5. Recheck after the change

    Review the action against the target. Update the current description only when you have evidence that the change happened.

Done when

Each target has a corresponding current entry and a visible next action if there’s a gap.

Watch for: Treating an intended safeguard as already in place.

Review a file access request

Practice separating who is asking, what they need, and what the policy permits.

Open the steps ＋

Use this paper exercise with a fictional class folder. Give a classmate a mock request to view a handout from a device described in the scenario.

  1. Name the request

    Write the requester, the device, the file, and the action. A request to view one handout is specific enough to assess.

  2. Check identity and device separately

    Read the mock policy and identify what it requires you to know about the requester and the device. Mark any missing evidence as unknown.

  3. Check authorization for that file

    Decide whether the requester’s role and reason meet the policy for the file. A verified identity answers who is asking. The policy answers whether they may access it.

  4. Choose the smallest permission

    If the mock policy permits access, choose the minimum permission needed for that request. For viewing a handout, that means read access to the handout.

  5. Test a changed request

    Change one detail, such as a request to edit the file or a different device. Apply the policy again and explain whether the decision changes.

Done when

You can explain the decision using the identity, device, resource, and permission required for this request.

Watch for: Using access to one file or presence on a trusted network as permission for every request.

Work through an ethical decision

Compare feasible choices when speed, accuracy, and responsibility pull in different directions.

Open the steps ＋

Use this classroom exercise when a fictional group discovers a mistake in the attendance totals just before submitting a report.

  1. Name the tension

    Write down the competing duties. The group wants to meet the deadline, and the person using the report needs an accurate total.

  2. Find the rules and duties

    Use the mock submission instructions to find who approves the report and what to do if a total is still being checked. Note any requirement you need to confirm.

  3. List feasible choices

    Compare options such as recounting before submission, filing a clearly marked provisional total if the procedure allows it, or asking the reviewer how to handle the deadline.

  4. Test the options

    Ask whether each option follows the rules, what happens to the people relying on the record, and what a responsible leader would be willing to stand behind.

  5. Decide and reflect

    Pick the option you can justify, say who needs to know, and explain what you’d do differently to prevent the same problem next time.

Done when

You can explain the choice using a rule, a likely consequence, and a duty or value.

Watch for: Choosing the fastest action before checking whose work depends on the record.

Study leadership through a short scene

Turn a film clip or classroom role-play into evidence you can discuss.

Open the steps ＋

Use this classroom exercise after a short scene that shows someone guiding a team through a problem.

  1. Pick one moment

    Choose a short exchange with a clear decision or interaction. Write one line about what the group needed at that point.

  2. Record the action

    Note the timestamp or scene cue and describe what the person said or did. For example, “She asked each group to explain its blocker before assigning a task.”

  3. Connect it to a competency

    Use the lesson’s leading, developing, and achieving categories. Explain which fits the action and how you can tell from the scene.

  4. Trace the effect

    Record what happened next, such as a teammate taking ownership or a timeline becoming clear. If the clip ends first, mark the effect as unknown.

  5. Share one observation

    Give a partner the scene cue, action, category, and evidence. Ask them which detail best supports the category you chose.

Done when

Your partner can find the same moment and understand why you chose that competency.

Watch for: Giving a general impression of the character without pointing to a specific action.

Study with a worked example

Try a classroom task first, then use the sample to see how the reasoning works.

Open the steps ＋

Use this method with an assigned classroom exercise that has a worked solution. Start with one part of the exercise if time is short.

  1. Pick a specific part to practice

    Choose a small output you can finish, such as the timeline or the format of a presentation. Read the requirement before opening the worked solution.

  2. Draft your answer first

    Write your own attempt and mark assumptions or places where you had to make a choice. Keep the draft so you can see what changed.

  3. Compare the reasoning

    Find the matching part of the sample. Read the notes explaining why the example works and look for the requirement each choice serves.

  4. Separate mistakes from valid differences

    Mark any mismatch with the stated requirement. If your answer differs from the sample but still meets the requirement, write one sentence defending your choice.

  5. Revise and explain

    Change the weakest part, then explain the revision aloud without reading from the sample. Use the handout reference beside the example if the reason remains unclear.

Done when

You can identify one change you made and the requirement or evidence behind it.

Watch for: Reading the sample first and treating its wording as the only correct answer.
