01The essentials

THE LESSON IN ONE IDEA

A voice plan states each requirement from the mission, gives every link a means that fits its terrain and threat, and backs each critical net with PACE levels that fail differently and with the resources to sustain them.

Explain each point in your own words. Then use the lesson page to check the details.

01

Requirements

Recall it, then reveal

Gather from the 0305 sources and state each requirement by users, mobility, reach, precedence, security, availability and quality.

Why it matters Only requirements that support a decision or an action are worth the scarce means.

Lesson source: page 1 · page 2

02

Means by category

Recall it, then reveal

Fixed (BONTEX, PANET, AFP FCS, DBTOCS), tactical (HF, VHF, UHF, CNR) and special purpose (video-conferencing, cellular, satellite phones).

Why it matters Each category has a different role; special purpose means are gap fillers within COMSEC policy.

Lesson source: page 2

03

Bands and line of sight

Recall it, then reveal

HF/SSB 3 to 30 MHz for long distance, VHF/FM 30 to 80 MHz for short distance, UHF/FM 403 to 433 MHz internal; hills block VHF and UHF.

Why it matters Terrain and distance decide whether a radio link works at all.

Lesson source: page 3

04

Connectivity hierarchy

Recall it, then reveal

PAM 6-00 0406 gives the AFP FCS as primary and HF/SSB as secondary on most links between command levels.

Why it matters It is the default routing plan the planner adapts and justifies.

Lesson source: page 3

05

Security and recovery

Recall it, then reveal

Frequency hopping and anti-jamming protect; compromised radios trigger a quick frequency change; the vigilance net uses ciphertext and double talk; the disaster net stays separate.

Why it matters The enemy can scan, jam and capture, and civil agencies cannot join the secured net.

Lesson source: page 3 · page 4 · page 5

06

Resources

Recall it, then reveal

Chart equipment, personnel and funds; use the SMTF for level 1 to 3 tactical radio repairs and SMMART for visits to units.

Why it matters A plan the unit cannot staff, fund or repair will not survive the operation.

Lesson source: page 5 · page 6

05Know the facts

Why voice matters
Despite computer networks and satellite communication, voice remains the bedrock of tactical communication (PAM 6-0102 0134). PAM 6-00 (0205) calls the communications system the voice of command.
Sources of requirements
Commander’s guidance or intent, staff guidance (staff are the users), higher, subordinate and adjacent headquarters, SOPs, and the CEOI and CESI (0305). The commander directs the means and the priorities.
CEOI and CESI
The CEOI says what to do; the CESI explains how to do it. Call signs and frequencies come from the CEOI, and how to use them from the CESI.
Signal estimate (METAL)
Mission, Enemy signal capabilities, Troops available, Area of operations, Logistical requirements. Also called the C4S estimate, and revised as the situation changes.
Three categories of communications
Fixed (BONTEX, PANET, AFP FCS, DBTOCS), tactical (HF, VHF and UHF radios, combat net radio with radio over IP) and special purpose (commercial video-conferencing, cellular and satellite phones).
Radio bands
HF/SSB 3 to 30 MHz for long-distance ground communication; VHF/FM 30 to 80 MHz for short distance; UHF/FM 403 to 433 MHz for internal communications.
Resources and maintenance
Chart equipment (0307), personnel (0308) and funds (0309). The SMTF repairs tactical HF and VHF radios up to level 3; SMMART visits brigades, battalions and companies.

Topic 1 of 5

Voice requirements and where they come from

The planner obtains requirements from the commander’s guidance or intent, staff guidance, higher, subordinate and adjacent headquarters, SOPs, and the CEOI and CESI (PAM 6-0102 0305). The commander directs the means and the priorities. The signal estimate, structured by METAL, examines every factor that may affect signal support. Each voice requirement is stated by net or service, users, mobility, reach, precedence, security, availability and quality, and recorded on the Annex A worksheet. A requirement is worth stating only if it supports a decision or an action.

  • The CEOI says what to do; the CESI explains how to do it.
  • Precedence answers who gets the net first when traffic is heavy.

Watch out: Listing nets because they existed last time, without asking which decision or task needs them.

Lesson source: page 1 · page 2

Topic 2 of 5

Fixed, tactical and special purpose means

Fixed communications are BONTEX, PANET, the AFP FCS and DBTOCS, and the Army is shifting telephone service to IP telephony on PANET. Tactical communications are HF, VHF and UHF radios and combat net radio using radio over IP and ad-hoc networks. Special purpose means (commercial video-conferencing, cellular and satellite phones) carry routine and administrative traffic and fill gaps, with prudence and within COMSEC policy. PAM 6-00 0406 sets a primary, secondary and tertiary means for each link between command levels.

  • HF/SSB is for long distance, VHF/FM for short distance, UHF/FM for internal communications.
  • Between strategic and operational headquarters the primary is the AFP FCS and the secondary HF/SSB.

Watch out: Choosing VHF for a link that crosses hills or open water, where line of sight fails.

Lesson source: page 2 · page 3

Topic 3 of 5

Spectrum, threat and security

Spectrum management plans, coordinates and manages spectrum use under NTC laws and policies (0218). Electronic warfare has support (sense), attack and protect parts, and protection includes frequency hopping and anti-jamming (0223). The vigilance net serves emergency and exigent situations and joint operations; it is less secure, so operators use ciphertext and double talk (0316). Radios may fall into enemy hands, so the plan must change frequencies quickly (0311d), and the cyber response team is employed where network attack is expected (0311c).

  • The disaster response net is unclassified and unsecured and must stay separate from the secured operational net.

Watch out: Bringing civil agencies or another service straight onto the secured command net.

Lesson source: page 3 · page 4 · page 5

Topic 4 of 5

The planning process, PACE and recovery

The signal planning process runs with the MDMP and troop leading procedures in seven steps: develop requirements (0305), determine available resources (0306 to 0309), develop priorities (0310), develop recovery procedures (0311), prepare a tentative plan (0312), finalize the plan (0313) and publish plans and orders (0314). The tentative plan sets primary, alternate, contingency and emergency means; the sample plan uses a Harris handheld VHF radio, a Harris manpack radio and a messenger. Each net needs a purpose, a net control station, members, primary and alternate frequencies and a break-in rule, and the plan is published as the C4S annex with appendices and tabs.

  • Choose each PACE level so it fails differently from the one above it.
  • In coalition operations each nation keeps a sovereign network, and the plan identifies points of integration.

Watch out: Calling a second radio on the same repeater an alternate.

Lesson source: page 4 · page 5

Topic 5 of 5

Voice resources and maintenance

The planner keeps an up-to-date chart of equipment (authorized TE, on hand, serviceable, unserviceable, reserve pool and capability), personnel (authorized TO, actual fill-up, training status, critical shortages, committed or deployed, reserve) and funds (installation, operation and maintenance of C4 systems and administrative requirements). The SMTF repairs tactical HF and VHF radios up to level 3 and UHF commercial radios up to level 2, programs radios and trains; SMMART repairs UHF commercial radios up to level 2, programs radios, gives refresher training and visits brigades, battalions and companies. Skills are perishable, and 22E, 22L and 22P are the relevant specialties.

  • The Army acquired the HF/SSB 20 W manpack and the VHF/FM 5 W handheld through the AFP modernization program.

Watch out: Counting authorized equipment or positions as if they were serviceable and filled.

Lesson source: page 5 · page 6

07Understand the reasoning

  • A requirement is worth stating only if it supports a decision or an action. Ask which information requirement or task needs the link, and what happens if it fails.
  • A PACE level is a real fallback only if it fails differently from the level above it. Two radios on the same repeater fail together.
  • Line of sight drives most voice choices: hills block VHF and UHF, repeaters on high ground extend them, and HF reaches over the horizon with variable conditions.

08Apply it

Fictional Philippine Army classroom exercise

In a fictional Philippine Army classroom exercise, a company moves into hills out of VHF line of sight of its command post, and a platoon operates on an island 30 km across open water. The enemy scans clear VHF and has started jamming the battalion VHF net.

Which means should the planner propose for the two links, and what must the plan add against the enemy?

Show the decision and reasoning

For the company in the hills, extend VHF with a repeater on high ground and keep HF as a fallback, because HF reaches over the horizon and does not fail with the repeater. For the island platoon, HF/SSB is the long-distance means, with special purpose means only as gap fillers within COMSEC policy. Against scanning, put encryption-capable radios on sensitive nets and limit what is said on clear nets. Against jamming, rely on electronic protection such as frequency hopping and anti-jamming, and keep a procedure to change frequencies quickly. Record each switch in the PACE table with its trigger.

09How to practice

  1. Gather voice requirements from the 0305 sources and state each one with its users, mobility, reach, precedence, security, availability and quality.
  2. Choose the means for each link from the fixed, tactical and special purpose categories, starting from the PAM 6-00 0406 table.
  3. Chart the equipment, personnel and funds available, then rank the requirements that can be supported.
  4. Write a PACE table whose levels fail differently, and add recovery procedures for compromised frequencies, jamming and network attack.
  5. Finalize with reconnaissance, propagation, coverage and gaps, then publish the voice appendix to the C4S annex.

10Check yourself

Which facility repairs level 3 HF radios, and which visits companies?

The SMTF repairs tactical HF and VHF radios up to level 3. SMMART visits brigades, battalions and companies.

What must a plan say about compromised frequencies?

It must include procedures to change frequencies quickly, because radios may fall into enemy hands (0311d).

Why must a disaster response net be separate from the operational net?

It is unclassified and unsecured and connects many entities, so it must be different from and separate to the Army’s secured operational net.
