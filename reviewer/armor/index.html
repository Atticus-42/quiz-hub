01Quick briefing

Distinguish the roles of mechanized infantry, cavalry, and tanks and assess the conditions that enable or limit armored mobility.

Read with Fundamentals of Armor Operations.

02Know the facts

Armor
A combined arms force designed for mounted combat using armor-protected vehicles as a principal means of accomplishing the mission.
Characteristics of armor
Protection, firepower, mobility, flexibility, intensive and flexible communications, and shock action.
Mechanized infantry
Combines a vehicle crew with a dismount team and can perform mounted or dismounted tasks according to mission and terrain.
Cavalry
Performs reconnaissance and surveillance and provides security, giving commanders information for decisions.
Tanks
Contribute protected mobility and firepower in a combined arms team and depend on support for sustained employment.
Mounted and dismounted employment
Mechanized infantry can operate with tanks when mounted and can work on terrain that requires movement on foot when dismounted.

03Build your understanding

Armor as a combined arms force

Armor uses protected vehicles as a principal means of mounted combat. The lesson connects its mission to fire, maneuver, and shock in coordination with other arms. It lists protection, firepower, mobility, flexibility, intensive and flexible communication, and shock action as characteristics. Mechanized infantry, cavalry, and tanks bring different capabilities to the combined effort.

  • Match the assigned task to the component's primary mission.
  • Explain why the other characteristics make mobility and firepower useful together.

Watch out: Treating all armored units as interchangeable because they use armored vehicles.

Lesson source: page 13 · page 14 · page 15 · page 16 · page 17 · page 18 · page 21

Mechanized infantry organization

The mechanized infantry mission is to close with the enemy by fire and maneuver, destroy or capture it, or repel assault by fire, close combat, and counterattack. A platoon has a headquarters and three mechanized infantry squads across four AIFVs. The carrier team normally comprises the carrier team leader, gunner, and driver. The dismount team is the remainder of the squad.

  • The headquarters rides in one AIFV and the three squads use the other three.
  • The carrier team operates the vehicle and its armament.

Watch out: Counting only the personnel on foot when evaluating the complete squad.

Lesson source: page 24 · page 25 · page 26 · page 27 · page 28

Mounted and dismounted capability

Mounted troops can use protected mobility and vehicle weapons, move with tanks, and engage light armor or dismounted elements under the conditions in the lesson. Dismounted troops can move through terrain vehicles cannot traverse, fix an enemy for maneuver, and secure obstacles. Dismounting introduces time and exposes personnel to small arms and indirect fire. Plans must account for approaches, weapon range, and support distance.

  • Consider where the vehicle can go and what troops must do on foot.
  • Include transition time and the limitations of both modes.

Watch out: Choosing mounted movement for a route that the vehicles cannot traverse.

Lesson source: page 29 · page 30 · page 31 · page 71

Mounting and dismounting as team procedures

The deck teaches disciplined movement at the vehicle door and assigns responsibility for the team to the squad or platoon leader. During mounting, maintain cover while others board, avoid crowding, and clear weapons and keep muzzles down once inside. Dismount examples distinguish terrain suitability from the direction of contact. Know which condition the vignette describes before using a diagram to identify the example.

  • The first to dismount is the last to mount under the taught SOP.
  • Use the stated conditions to identify the applicable demonstration.

Watch out: Assuming the same diagram applies to every terrain or contact condition.

Lesson source: page 32 · page 33 · page 34 · page 35 · page 36 · page 41 · page 46

Cavalry and economy of force

Cavalry conducts reconnaissance, surveillance, and security in close operations. It provides situational awareness and helps conserve the main force for concentration at the decisive place and time. The deck lists four basic organizational designs, including a separate troop or company operating for its brigade. Suitable terrain supports cavalry's mobility. Obstacles, rugged terrain, logistics, and distance from the main body can limit its effectiveness.

  • Cavalry develops awareness and helps protect the supported force's maneuver.
  • Plan range, supporting fires, terrain, and distance from the main body.

Watch out: Assigning cavalry an enemy tank engagement simply because it is mounted.

Lesson source: page 53 · page 54 · page 55 · page 56 · page 60 · page 62 · page 63 · page 72

Tank operations and combined arms planning

Tank units close with and neutralize enemy forces through fire, maneuver, and shock in coordination with other arms. Accurate direct and anti-tank fire and thermal sights support the combined arms team. Light tanks provide less protection than main battle tanks and remain vulnerable to anti-armor weapons. Close terrain restricts mobility and firepower. The slides call for direct-fire controls, support-by-fire positions, air protection, and sustainment from supporting organizations.

  • Check terrain, weapon employment, supporting fires, and sustainment together.
  • The deck distinguishes tank planning against enemy armor from mechanized infantry and cavalry planning.

Watch out: Treating armor protection as immunity or assuming infantry can organically meet every tank sustainment requirement.

Identify the equipment by configuration

The recognition slides include several configurations sharing the M113A2 name, including an armored personnel carrier, a remote-controlled weapon system, an IFV with unmanned turret, and an ambulance configuration. Other named examples include the armored infantry fighting vehicle, mounted mortar system, Commando V-150, Simba, fire support vehicle, Sabrah light tank on ASCOD II, and Guarani wheeled APC. Read the complete label and distinguish the role or configuration asked about.

  • Identify the variant rather than relying on the shared hull designation.
  • The Guarani shown is a 6×6 wheeled APC and the Sabrah shown is a light tank.

Watch out: Treating the APC, ambulance, and IFV configurations as the same answer because they share a family name.

04Understand the reasoning

  • Nominal vehicle speed tells you little about the actual movement timeline if road access, bridge limits, or recovery support are unresolved.
  • Armor protection reduces exposure to some hazards. Planning still needs to consider terrain, communications, sustainment, and tasks that require people on foot.
  • When a case presents a capable platform, check whether the terrain, support, and assigned role let it deliver that capability.

05Apply it

Fictional Philippine Army classroom exercise

A Philippine Army classroom group is comparing vehicles for a training demonstration. A tracked option offers greater protection, while a lighter wheeled option has confirmed access to the approved venue. The course includes a task participants must perform on foot.

What information should drive the choice of platform for this demonstration?

Show the decision and reasoning

Start with the demonstration objective, then compare verified route access, available support, crew readiness, and how participants will perform the dismounted task. The lighter option fits a task that requires assured access if it can meet the learning objective. The tracked option may make sense if the exercise requires that capability and the venue can support it.
