01The essentials

THE LESSON IN ONE IDEA

Company command combines authority, decisions, and leadership with accountable management of people, training, information, and property.

06Build your understanding

Command, leadership, and the company standard

Company command combines leadership and management with formal authority. The art of command joins authority, decision-making, and leadership. As the company standard, the commander models character, presence, and intellect and applies leading, developing, and achieving. Staff and subordinate leaders help implement decisions while the commander remains engaged in the outcome.

  • Authority enables decisions and direction.
  • Leading, developing, and achieving describe the commander's actions.

Watch out: Treating appointment or rank as proof that the leader has set the standard.

Lesson source: page 16 · page 17 · page 19 · page 20 · page 21 · page 22 · page 23

Discipline and personnel stewardship

The company commander protects soldiers' rights and applies standards based on military laws and regulations. Personnel stewardship includes completing actions, reading reports, maintaining records, and considering eligible soldiers for recognition fairly. Daily two-way communication and engagement expose personnel concerns before they erode confidence or affect readiness.

  • Base discipline on the applicable standard and protect rights.
  • Verify completion of personnel actions and avoid favoritism.

Watch out: Treating a report forwarded for action as evidence the action was completed.

Lesson source: page 27 · page 28 · page 32 · page 35

Mission-focused training

The commander uses the Mission Essential Task List to identify what the unit must be ready to do. Performance-oriented training gives soldiers a chance to perform and demonstrate competence. Decentralizing execution puts training in the hands of leaders closest to the work, while sustainment training preserves capability after the initial event. Protect time for mission priorities by minimizing training detractors.

  • Use the METL to select priority performance.
  • Observe soldiers doing the task and plan sustainment.

Watch out: Treating attendance or hours delivered as proof that the unit can perform.

Lesson source: page 29 · page 40

Supply, maintenance, and turnover

The commander maintains, secures, and accounts for unit property. A joint physical inventory with the outgoing commander compares actual items against the record at turnover. Key discrepancies warrant personal involvement, follow-up, and review of gain and loss documents. Maintenance also requires assigned responsibilities and operators trained to care for equipment.

  • Physical verification and record reconciliation serve different purposes.
  • Keep records current as property is gained or lost.

Watch out: Accepting a clean-looking list before checking an unexplained physical shortfall.

Lesson source: page 30 · page 38

Intelligence, operations, and civil-military functions

Company intelligence work separates critical facts from assumptions, evaluates information, and routes significant confirmed intelligence to higher headquarters. Operations work uses that information and the commander's intent to plan, prepare, supervise, and review execution. Civil-military operations use command guidance and measures of effectiveness to see whether actions are achieving the intended effect among audiences.

  • Intelligence assesses what is known and uncertain.
  • Operations verifies that tasks are understood, supervised, and accomplished.

Watch out: Passing an unconfirmed inference off as a verified operational fact.

Lesson source: page 24 · page 31 · page 36 · page 37 · page 39

Command habits and FIRST

The slides link leading from the front, moral courage, teamwork, care for soldiers, and timely decisions to a professional unit. The FIRST mnemonic highlights faith, increasing knowledge, a reliable C3 system, positive staff relationships, and time management. In practice, capable staff, reliable communication, and disciplined priorities support the commander's decisions and follow-through.

  • Moral courage is acting for a moral reason despite possible adverse consequences.
  • Use staff relationships and time management to support decisions and execution.

Watch out: Confusing a prompt decision with skipping the facts needed for it.

Lesson source: page 44 · page 45 · page 46 · page 47 · page 48 · page 49 · page 50

07Understand the reasoning

  • Delegating a record check gives someone the work and authority to verify it. The commander remains accountable for decisions that rely on the result.
  • A training event can be well attended and still leave the mission task unproven. Observe performance to determine what needs another practice cycle.
  • When reviewing a company problem, identify the functional lead and the company commander's responsibility for the outcome.

08Apply it

Fictional Philippine Army classroom exercise

At a Philippine Army company turnover, a training aid shown on the inventory sheet cannot be found, and the next morning's class expects to use it.

How should the incoming commander handle accountability and training continuity?

Show the decision and reasoning

Reconcile the discrepancy with the outgoing commander and the applicable records. Record the item as unresolved until verified. Assign follow-up and communicate the effect on the class. An approved substitute or revised sequence can preserve the learning objective while the property discrepancy receives proper attention.

09How to practice

  1. Compare the current property record with the items presented during the joint inventory.
  2. Record each discrepancy and confirm which supporting record or custodian can help resolve it.
  3. Assign follow-up and identify any upcoming tasks affected by the discrepancy.
  4. Review the result before treating the item as accounted for, and brief any remaining effect on training.

Review the steps with Company Management (Company Level).
