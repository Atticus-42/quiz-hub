01The essentials

THE LESSON IN ONE IDEA

A data plan starts from the information exchanges the mission needs, matches each link to its terrain, spectrum and threat, protects every critical service, voice included, with independent PACE paths and recovery, and sizes the links that carry voice.

Explain each point in your own words. Then use the lesson page to check the details.

01

Part of the signal plan

Recall it, then reveal

The data plan keeps the commander connected and is part of the signal plan, published with the C4S annex; it is not a separate IT project.

Why it matters It must follow the same planning process and priorities as the rest of signal support.

Lesson source: page 1 · page 9

02

Requirements first

Recall it, then reveal

State who exchanges what, with whom, how quickly, how securely and over what terrain; equipment comes later as a resource.

Why it matters Requirements drawn from equipment miss what the mission actually needs.

Lesson source: page 3

03

The section 2.5 test

Recall it, then reveal

Each requirement must be specific, measurable, tied to the mission or CCIR, ranked in priority and phased.

Why it matters Resources are always limited, so only clear, ranked requirements can be allocated.

Lesson source: page 6

04

Technology by environment

Recall it, then reveal

Over water use HF, satellite or relays; in mountains repeaters and a propagation study; in foliage clear-sky siting; in cities short hops with backup; against EW frequency hopping and anti-jamming.

Why it matters No single technology works everywhere.

Lesson source: page 7

05

Recovery

Recall it, then reveal

Plan for interrupted connectivity, EW, cyberattack and compromised frequencies, executed by the TOC team, C3 restoration team, network recovery team and CSIRT.

Why it matters Connect and sustain are not enough without a planned way to recover.

Lesson source: page 10

06

Voice on data

Recall it, then reveal

List voice services that depend on the network, size them (calls × per-call figure + data, + at least 25 percent), set a priority rule and keep an independent fallback.

Why it matters A voice plan is only as reliable as the network under it.

05Know the facts

C4S
Command and Control Communications, and Cyber Systems: the capabilities that enable signal. Its four sub-systems are communications, electronics, information and cyber (PAM 6-00 0127, 0401).
Connect, Sustain, Recover
Connect gives connectivity to all Army units, Sustain keeps it going through the operation, and Recover repairs and restores it when attacked or destroyed (0144).
Data transmission requirement
A statement of who must exchange what information, with whom, how quickly, how securely and over what terrain. It comes from the mission and the commander, not from the equipment on hand.
Requirement categories
Reliability and redundancy, security, speed and capacity, interoperability, scalability, coverage and range, mobility, and environmental resilience and sustainment.
PANET and the AFP FCS
PANET is the secured, managed Army intranet, with its primary hub at the PA communications center, Fort Bonifacio. The AFP FCS is the transmission superhighway between switches, run by GHQ-AFP.
Recovery teams
The TOC team, the C3 restoration team, the network recovery team and the cyber security incident response team (CSIRT) execute recovery (0143, 0228 to 0231).
Voice sizing figures
About 80 to 90 kbps per uncompressed G.711 call and about 25 to 30 kbps per compressed G.729 call, plus the data, plus a margin of at least 25 percent.

Topic 1 of 5

Foundations: C4S, environments and principles

A data communications plan keeps the commander connected to every unit and information source the mission needs. It is part of the signal plan. C4S enables signal and has communications, electronics, information and cyber sub-systems. The plan works in three environments: physical (islands, mountains, foliage, cities, typhoons), cyberspace (physical, logical and people domains) and the electromagnetic spectrum. It must answer information warfare, hybrid, cyber and insider threats, and follow eight principles: operations-focused, interoperable, redundant, scalable, secured, reliability first, survivability and appropriate technology.

  • Communications must never be the limiting factor on the commander’s mission.
  • The newest technology is not necessarily the best choice.

Watch out: Treating the data plan as a separate IT project instead of part of the signal plan.

Lesson source: page 1 · page 2 · page 3

Topic 2 of 5

Data transmission requirements

A requirement states who must exchange what information, with whom, how quickly, how securely and over what terrain. Sources are the commander’s guidance or intent, staff guidance, higher, subordinate and adjacent units, the SOP, and the CEOI and CESI (0305). The signal estimate uses METAL. PAM 6-00 (0211) lists seven C2 requirements, each implying a data requirement, and PAM 3-00 adds information qualities and the CCIR. Each requirement is written under the eight categories, quantified on the Annex C worksheet, and tested as specific, measurable, tied to the mission or CCIR, ranked and phased.

  • Information that arrives late, or in a form no one can read, has the same effect as none.
  • Total the volume by link on the Annex C worksheet.

Watch out: Starting the requirements list from the equipment on hand.

Lesson source: page 3 · page 4 · page 5 · page 6

Topic 3 of 5

Choosing data technologies

Fixed communications (BONTEX, PANET, AFP FCS, DBTOCS) are the permanent backbone. Tactical communications work on the move and on the halt over the radio spectrum, including CNR with radio over IP and ad-hoc networks. Special purpose means (VSAT, mobile satellite, cellular, satellite phones, commercial video-conferencing) reach where the fixed system cannot and are used with utmost prudence under COMSEC policy. The PAM 6-00 0406 table is the default routing plan, and the environment drives each choice: HF, satellite or relays over water, repeaters and a propagation study in mountains, clear-sky siting in foliage, short hops in cities.

  • Commercial cellular is for routine or administrative traffic, never classified.
  • A typical Army network is a hybrid: a star at the TOC and a mesh in the field.

Watch out: Using one technology for every link, or ignoring a means’ stated limitation.

Lesson source: page 6 · page 7 · page 8 · page 9

Topic 4 of 5

Building the plan: process, PACE and recovery

The plan follows the seven-step signal planning process (0304 to 0314), alongside the MDMP or troop leading procedures. The tentative plan sets the concept with PACE, allocates resources, sites C4S centers, sets security measures and plans interconnection and alternate routing. The finalization checklist confirms requirements, practicality, priorities, areas of responsibility, a site survey and propagation study, locations, coverage and gaps, and security. Recovery covers interrupted connectivity, electronic warfare, cyberattack and compromised frequencies, executed by the TOC team, the C3 restoration team, the network recovery team and the CSIRT. Joint operations need interoperability, coalition operations points of integration, and HADR a separate, unclassified net.

  • The signal plan has five parts: situation, mission, execution, sustainment, and command and signal.
  • The handout suggests a data communications appendix, using the Annex D template.

Watch out: Connecting the HADR net to the secured operational net.

Lesson source: page 9 · page 10 · page 11

Topic 5 of 5

Voice on data networks

More voice now travels as data: IP telephony over PANET is replacing BONTEX, CNR links radio nets with radio over IP, and secure voice and video-teleconferencing run over the C4ISTAR network. A voice-on-data plan determines which voice services depend on the network, the capacity they need, a priority rule, a fallback path that does not share the same failure point, security, dependencies such as power and switches, and recovery. To size a link, multiply simultaneous calls by the per-call figure, add the data, and add a margin of at least 25 percent.

  • Worked example: (15 × 30 + 400) × 1.25 ≈ 1,060 kbps, so plan about 1.1 Mbps or more.
  • The PA default fallback order is AFP FCS, then HF/SSB, then VHF/UHF, then commercial.

Watch out: Sizing the link for data only, so voice quality fails at peak.

Lesson source: page 12 · page 13

07Understand the reasoning

  • Requirements come first and equipment second. Start from the information exchanges the mission needs, then see what the resources can support and rank the rest.
  • Once voice rides on a data network, the voice plan is only as reliable as that network, including its power, antennas, switches and operations center.
  • No single technology works everywhere, so each link is matched to its terrain, spectrum and threat, and its fallback must not share the same failure point.

08Apply it

Fictional Philippine Army classroom exercise

In a fictional Philippine Army classroom exercise, a task force TOC reaches higher headquarters through one microwave link. A battalion operates on an island across open water, and the higher headquarters now wants a daily video-teleconference and drone video from the TOC.

What should the data plan change, and how should the TOC-to-higher link be checked?

Show the decision and reasoning

One microwave link fails the redundant and survivability principles, so the plan adds an independent path, such as a satellite terminal, and keeps HF/SSB as the secondary means of the PAM 6-00 0406 table. The island battalion needs HF, satellite or relay stations because line of sight fails over water. The new video and VTC traffic is stated as requirements with volume, delay, precedence and classification, then the link is sized: voice calls times the per-call figure, plus data, plus at least 25 percent margin. If the link is short, reduce the load at peak hours or ask for more capacity.

09How to practice

  1. List each information exchange requirement with originator, recipient, information type, volume and frequency, maximum delay, precedence, classification and phase.
  2. Check that each requirement is specific, measurable, tied to the mission or CCIR, ranked and phased.
  3. Choose the means for each link from the 0406 table and the environment responses, and note each means’ limits.
  4. Write the PACE table for each critical service, including voice, so that no two levels share a failure point, and add recovery procedures for each contingency.
  5. Size the links that carry voice and data, finalize against the checklist, and attach the data communications appendix to the signal plan.

10Check yourself

Name four sources of data transmission requirements for the signal planner.

Any four of: commander’s guidance or intent, staff guidance, higher, subordinate and adjacent units, the SOP, and the CEOI and CESI.

What must the plan include for compromised frequencies after captured equipment?

Procedures to change frequencies efficiently, in the shortest possible time (0311).

Why is a voice service that runs over IP not made safe just by having a backup router?

Power, antennas, switches and the network operations center must also survive, and the fallback voice path must not share the same failure point.

11Objective & analytical practice

Answer first, then reveal the explanation. Situations are fictional classroom exercises applying the lesson.

objectiveWhat does the mnemonic METAL stand for, and what is the signal estimate used for?

Answer: Mission, enemy signal capabilities, troops available, area of operations and logistical requirements; it is an orderly examination of every factor that may affect the signal mission.

The estimate is revised as the situation changes, and its full format is in Annex A.

Lesson source: page 4

objectiveFor a link between an operational and a tactical headquarters, what are the primary and secondary means under PAM 6-00 0406?

Answer: Primary AFP FCS if technically feasible; secondary HF/SSB with VHF/FM or UHF/FM, linked through repeaters.

The 0406 table is the default routing plan, and changes must be justified.

Lesson source: page 7

objectiveList the seven steps of the signal planning process.

Answer: Develop requirements, determine available resources, develop priorities, develop recovery procedures, prepare a tentative plan, finalize the plan, publish plans and orders.

The steps run from paragraph 0305 to paragraph 0314.

Lesson source: page 9 · page 10

analyticalA brigade TOC needs 20 simultaneous compressed voice calls and 500 kbps of data over one link. Estimate the link size with a 25 percent margin.

Answer: Assuming about 30 kbps per call: (20 × 30 + 500) × 1.25 = (600 + 500) × 1.25 ≈ 1,375 kbps, about 1.4 Mbps.

A compressed G.729 call needs about 25 to 30 kbps including overhead; the margin covers peaks and overhead.

Lesson source: page 13 · page 19

analyticalHow do you keep the HADR radio net from compromising the secured operational net?

Answer: Keep it separate from the secured net, treat it as unclassified and unsecured, and never connect the two.

Connecting the HADR net to the secured net is listed as a common planning error.

Lesson source: page 11 · page 13

analyticalWhy does an information requirement need a maximum delay and a precedence, not only a volume?

Answer: Because late or unreadable information has the same effect as none, and because limited resources force the planner to rank requirements.

Delay tests timeliness; precedence decides what is supported first when capacity is short.

Lesson source: page 4 · page 6
